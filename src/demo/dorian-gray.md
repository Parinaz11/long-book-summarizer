# The Picture of Dorian Gray

## Book overview

The novel tells the story of Dorian Gray, a remarkably handsome young man whose portrait is painted by the artist Basil Hallward. Captivated by the cynical and hedonistic philosophy of Lord Henry Wotton, Dorian becomes infatuated with his own beauty and makes a tragic wish to trade places with the canvas so that he may remain eternally young while the portrait ages and bears the burden of his sins. Dorian falls deeply in love with a young actress named Sibyl Vane, but after she delivers a poor performance out of love for him, he cruelly rejects her, driving her to suicide. Noticing the first cruel change in the portrait, Dorian hides it in his old schoolroom. Over the years, Dorian plunges into a life of secret vice, degradation, and corruption, heavily influenced by a mysterious yellow book. Despite vicious rumors surrounding his absences and the ruin of others, his youthful appearance shields him from suspicion. When Basil Hallward confronts him about his vile reputation and demands to see the state of his soul, Dorian shows him the hideous, aging portrait. In a fit of irrational hatred, Dorian murders Basil and later blackmails Alan Campbell into destroying the body with acid. Although Dorian believes he is safe after James Vane—Sibyl's brother who had vowed to kill him—accidentally dies in a shooting hunt, his guilt and terror mount. Seeking a final, superficial redemption by sparing a village girl, Dorian goes to check the portrait, hoping to find it improved. Instead, he sees increased cruelty and bloodstains. Desperate to destroy the evidence of his crimes, Dorian stabs the painting with the same knife he used to kill Basil. His servants enter to find a pristine portrait of the young Dorian alongside a dead, withered, and unrecognizable old man.

**Themes:** The duality of art and life | The destructive nature of hedonism | The pursuit of eternal youth and beauty | Moral corruption and conscience | The corrupting influence of others

**Main characters:**
- Dorian Gray: A remarkably handsome young man whose wish for eternal youth leads to his moral corruption and ultimate ruin.
- Lord Henry Wotton: A cynical, witty aristocrat who captivates Dorian with his hedonistic philosophy of youth and pleasure.
- Basil Hallward: An artist deeply infatuated with Dorian who paints the fateful portrait and is ultimately murdered by him.
- Sibyl Vane: A talented young actress who falls in love with Dorian and tragically commits suicide after he rejects her.
- James Vane: Sibyl's protective brother who swears to kill the man who ruined his sister and tracks Dorian for years.

## Chapter 1: The Artist and His Fascinating Portrait

**Chapter summary:** In his artist's studio, Basil Hallward contemplates a stunning full-length portrait of a remarkably handsome young man named Dorian Gray. Lord Henry Wotton, a cynical and witty visitor, admires the painting and urges Basil to exhibit it at the Grosvenor. Basil refuses, explaining that he has put too much of himself into the work and that it reveals the secret of his own soul. Pressured by Lord Henry, Basil recounts how he met Dorian Gray at a reception hosted by Lady Brandon, describing how an intense, immediate fascination overtook him and profoundly influenced his art and perception of life. Lord Henry remains intrigued and expresses a strong desire to meet Dorian. When the butler announces that Dorian Gray has arrived at the studio, Basil explicitly asks Lord Henry not to try to influence or spoil the young man, whom Basil regards as essential to his art and happiness. Ignoring this plea, Lord Henry insists on meeting him and leads Basil into the house.

- **Section 1: Admiration of the Portrait** — Basil Hallward admires his finished portrait of a beautiful young man in his studio, while Lord Henry Wotton smokes and urges him to exhibit it at the Grosvenor.
- **Section 2: The Reason for Secrecy** — Basil discusses the fatality of physical and intellectual distinction and explains to Lord Henry that he refuses to exhibit the portrait because it reveals the secret of his own soul.
- **Section 3: The Story of Meeting Dorian Gray** — Basil recounts his fateful first meeting with Dorian Gray at Lady Brandon's reception, explaining how Dorian's personality completely transformed his artistic vision and became vital to his life.
- **Section 4: The Arrival of Dorian Gray** — The butler announces that Dorian Gray has arrived at the studio, prompting Lord Henry to demand an introduction despite Basil's earnest plea not to influence the young man.

## Chapter 2: Lord Henry Influences Dorian Gray at the Studio

**Chapter summary:** In Basil Hallward's studio, Dorian Gray meets Lord Henry Wotton, who captivates the young man with his cynical and hedonistic philosophy about the supreme value of youth and beauty. While Basil paints, Lord Henry talks to Dorian in the garden, planting seeds of self-awareness and vanity. When the portrait is finally finished, Dorian is struck by his own beauty and falls in love with the canvas. Overcome by the realization that he will age while the portrait remains eternally young, Dorian desperately wishes to trade places with the artwork, declaring he would give his soul for eternal youth. Stung by Lord Henry's influence and Dorian's sudden bitterness, Basil attempts to destroy the canvas, but Dorian stops him and claims the portrait as his own. Despite Basil's pleas to stay, Dorian chooses to dine and go to the theatre with Lord Henry, leaving the painter heartbroken and alone while Dorian embarks on a new, impressionable path.

- **Section 1: Introduction of Lord Henry and Dorian Gray** — Dorian Gray arrives at Basil's studio and meets Lord Henry Wotton, who immediately begins to fascinate the young man.
- **Section 2: Lord Henry's Philosophy on Youth and Self-Development** — While posing for the portrait, Dorian listens to Lord Henry expound on the dangers of self-denial and the importance of living life fully through the senses.
- **Section 3: The Portrait is Finished and Dorian's Jealousy of Youth** — Basil finishes the masterpiece, and Dorian is struck by his own beauty, immediately falling into despair over the realization that he will grow old while the painting stays young.
- **Section 4: Plans for the Theatre and Parting Ways** — Dorian decides to accompany Lord Henry to the theatre despite Basil's earnest pleas to stay, leaving Basil deeply distressed.

## Chapter 3: Lord Henry Learns Dorian's Origins and Captivates Him

**Chapter summary:** Lord Henry Wotton visits his uncle, Lord Fermor, to uncover the background of Dorian Gray, learning of the tragic romance and death of Dorian's mother, Margaret Devereux. Intrigued by this dark lineage and the immense potential for exerting his influence over the impressionable young man, Lord Henry attends a luncheon at his Aunt Agatha's home. There, he dominates the conversation with witty paradoxes and a hedonistic philosophy of youth, pleasure, and the repetition of follies, all while holding Dorian Gray utterly spellbound. Following the meal, Mr. Erskine expresses admiration for Lord Henry's brilliance while warning him of his dangerous nature. As the guests disperse, Dorian forsakes his plans with Basil Hallward and begs to accompany Lord Henry to the park, eager to listen to more of his mesmerizing talk.

- **Section 1: Lord Henry Visits Lord Fermor** — Lord Henry calls on his cynical uncle, Lord Fermor, to gather information about the family history of his new acquaintance, Dorian Gray.
- **Section 2: Reflections on Dorian's Heritage and Influence** — Walking to his aunt's house, Lord Henry reflects upon Dorian's tragic parentage and resolves to dominate the young man's mind completely.
- **Section 3: The Luncheon at Aunt Agatha's** — At a luncheon in Berkeley Square, Lord Henry dazzles the guests, including Dorian Gray, with brilliant paradoxes and a philosophy centered on pleasure and youth.
- **Section 4: Dorian Joins Lord Henry** — Mr. Erskine warns Lord Henry that he is dangerous, and as the guests leave, Dorian insists on accompanying Lord Henry to the park instead of visiting Basil Hallward.

## Chapter 4: Dorian Falls in Love with an Actress

**Chapter summary:** One month later, Dorian Gray visits Lord Henry at his Mayfair home, where he briefly encounters Lord Henry's wife, Victoria. After she leaves, Dorian confesses to Lord Henry that he has fallen deeply in love with an actress named Sibyl Vane, whom he watches perform Shakespeare every night in a squalid east-end theatre. Dorian describes how he wandered into the shabby theatre and was completely captivated by Sibyl's extraordinary beauty, genius, and voice, viewing her as a divine embodiment of all theatrical heroines. Lord Henry is amused and fascinated by Dorian's intense, complex passion, viewing the lad as a creation of his own influence and a rich subject for psychological study. Although Dorian is furious when Lord Henry speaks cynically about women and romance, he eagerly invites Lord Henry and Basil Hallward to join him the next evening to witness Sibyl's performance as Juliet. Later that night, after Lord Henry contemplates the beauty, danger, and experimental nature of human passions, he receives a telegram announcing that Dorian and Sibyl are engaged to be married.

- **Section 1: A Visit to Lord Henry's House and Lady Henry** — Dorian Gray waits in Lord Henry's library and is briefly greeted by Lord Henry's untidy and sentimental wife, Lady Henry, before Lord Henry finally arrives.
- **Section 2: Dorian Confesses His Love for Sibyl Vane** — Dorian tells Lord Henry about his discovery of a humble east-end theatre and his profound, worshipful love for the young actress Sibyl Vane.
- **Section 3: Planning the Theater Outing** — Lord Henry agrees to accompany Dorian to see Sibyl act the following night alongside Basil Hallward, while Lord Henry reflects on Dorian's development.
- **Section 4: The Engagement Telegram** — Returning home late at night, Lord Henry finds a telegram informing him that Dorian Gray has become engaged to Sibyl Vane.

## Chapter 5: Sibyl Vane's Engagement and James's Departure

**Chapter summary:** In their dingy sitting-room, Sibyl Vane ecstatically tells her mother and her departing brother, James, about her engagement to her wealthy lover, whom she calls Prince Charming. While Mrs. Vane focuses on the financial prospects of the match and her own theatrical self-drama, James is filled with a protective and violent distrust of the unknown dandy courting his sister. Before leaving for Australia, James insists on taking a walk with Sibyl through the park, during which she briefly catches sight of Dorian Gray driving past in a carriage. Unable to see him, James vows that if the stranger ever harms his sister, he will track him down and kill him. Back at home before his departure, James confronts his mother and forces her to admit that she and her late partner were never married. Enraged by the dishonour and fearful for Sibyl's safety, James repeats his murderous oath to protect his sister before departing for his sea-faring life, leaving Mrs. Vane to indulge in theatrical sentimentality over the missed dramatic potential of the moment.

- **Section 1: Sibyl's Joy and James's Return** — Sibyl excitedly confesses her profound happiness and love for her unnamed prince to her mother, just as her rugged brother James returns home and announces his upcoming departure for Australia.
- **Section 2: A Walk in the Park and a Glimpse of Prince Charming** — James and Sibyl walk together toward the park, where Sibyl spots Dorian Gray driving by in a carriage; though James misses seeing him, he fiercely vows to kill the stranger if he ever wrongs Sibyl.
- **Section 3: The Confession and James's Departure** — After returning home, James forces his mother to admit the truth about her unmarried past, swears a violent oath to protect his sister from any betrayal, and finally leaves for his journey to Australia.

## Chapter 6: Dinner Discussion Regarding Dorian Gray's Engagement

**Chapter summary:** At dinner at the Bristol, Lord Henry informs a startled Basil Hallward that Dorian Gray is engaged to be married to a young actress named Sibyl Vane. Basil expresses dismay, worrying that the match will ruin Dorian's high social standing, while Lord Henry remains characteristically detached and amused by the development, viewing it as an interesting psychological study. Dorian soon arrives, flushed with excitement and glowing with happiness, and recounts how he fell in love with Sibyl's breathtaking stage performances and how they became engaged. Dorian declares his pure devotion to Sibyl, asserting that her trust and love make him good and temporarily insulate him from Lord Henry's cynical, poisonous theories about life and pleasure. Lord Henry and Basil debate individualism, morality, pleasure, and the nature of women. Afterward, the trio prepares to leave for the theatre to see Sibyl perform, leaving Basil with a deep, melancholy sense of loss that Dorian will never be entirely his again.

- **Section 1: Lord Henry Breaks the News to Basil** — Lord Henry informs a surprised Basil Hallward at dinner that Dorian Gray has become engaged to a young actress, sparking a debate between the two men about the wisdom and social implications of the match.
- **Section 2: Dorian Arrives and Recounts His Romance** — An excited Dorian Gray arrives at the dinner, enthusiastically describing his love for Sibyl Vane, her theatrical brilliance, and their romantic engagement.
- **Section 3: Debating Pleasure and Heading to the Theatre** — Lord Henry and Basil debate theories of pleasure, morality, and women while Dorian defends his pure love, after which the three men depart for the theatre to watch Sibyl act.

## Chapter 7: Sibyl Vane's Tragic Failure and the Altered Portrait

**Chapter summary:** Dorian Gray, Lord Henry, and Basil Hallward attend the theatre to see Sibyl Vane perform as Juliet. To Dorian's horror and his companions' disappointment, Sibyl acts with complete artificiality and failure, having lost her passion because her real love for Dorian has made her see the emptiness of stage illusion. After the play, Dorian cruelly rejects her, telling her he will never see her again. He wanders the dawn-lit streets of London before returning home, where he notices a subtle, cruel change in Basil's portrait of him. Remembering the mad wish he made at Basil's studio, Dorian realizes his wish for the portrait to bear the burden of his sins has somehow come true. Overwhelmed by remorse and pity for the canvas, he resolves to reform, reject Lord Henry's cynical philosophies, make amends with Sibyl, and marry her. He conceals the portrait behind a screen and steps out into the fresh morning air, trying to rekindle his love for Sibyl.

- **Section 1: Sibyl Vane's Disastrous Performance** — Dorian, Lord Henry, and Basil watch Sibyl Vane perform in Romeo and Juliet, but her acting is terribly artificial and a complete failure, shocking and boring the audience.
- **Section 2: Dorian's Cruel Rejection and Wandering** — Dorian goes backstage and harshly breaks off his engagement with Sibyl, who tries to explain that her love for him ruined her acting; after leaving her sobbing, Dorian wanders the streets of London until dawn.
- **Section 3: The Altered Portrait and Dorian's Resolution** — Returning home, Dorian discovers a cruel expression on Basil's portrait of him, realizing his old wish has come true; stricken with remorse, he resolves to change his ways, marry Sibyl, and hide the cursed painting.

## Chapter 8: The Discovery of Sibyl Vane's Death

**Chapter summary:** Dorian Gray wakes up late and feels completely happy until he notices the cruel change in Basil's portrait. Moved by remorse, he writes a passionate letter of apology to Sibyl Vane, intending to marry her and make amends. Lord Henry arrives and breaks the news that Sibyl has committed suicide by poison in her dressing-room after the disastrous play. Though initially devastated, Dorian is quickly influenced by Lord Henry's cynical philosophy, which frames Sibyl's tragic death as a beautiful artistic masterpiece rather than a real-life horror. Dorian rationalizes his lack of deep grief and realizes that he is not truly heartless, but rather detached from vulgar reality, viewing the event as a Greek tragedy in which he was merely a spectator. He decides that the portrait will bear the burden of his sins and age in his place, leaving him free to pursue eternal youth, beauty, and secret pleasures without consequence. After covering the canvas with the screen, Dorian goes to join Lord Henry at the opera.

- **Section 1: Dorian Discovers the Altered Portrait** — Dorian wakes up refreshed and happy, but upon examining the portrait behind the screen, he confirms that it has indeed changed to reflect his cruelty toward Sibyl Vane.
- **Section 2: Lord Henry Brings Tragic News** — Dorian resolves to marry Sibyl to make amends, but Lord Henry arrives and shatters his plans by revealing that Sibyl has died from poison in her dressing-room.
- **Section 3: Consuming the Tragedy as Art** — Lord Henry persuades Dorian to view Sibyl's death not as a crude tragedy, but as a wonderful artistic work, convincing Dorian to shed his remorse and embrace a life of eternal youth while the portrait ages.

## Chapter 9: Basil Visits Dorian and Makes a Confession

**Chapter summary:** The morning after Sibyl Vane's suicide, Basil Hallward visits Dorian Gray, horrified to find him seemingly callous, unaffected, and cheerful after attending the opera. Basil blames Lord Henry Wotton's cynical influence for Dorian's apparent lack of heart. When Basil approaches the screen covering his portrait of Dorian, Dorian flies into a panic and threatens to end their friendship if Basil looks at it. Determined to stop Basil from exhibiting the painting in Paris, Dorian attempts to force Basil's secret by demanding to know why he previously refused to show it. In response, Basil confesses his intense, romantic idolatry for Dorian, explaining that he poured too much of his soul into the artwork. Relieved to realize Basil's secret has nothing to do with the painting's supernatural corruption, Dorian refuses to let him see it or ever sit for him again. After Basil departs sadly, Dorian reflects on the tragic nature of the painter's obsession and resolves to securely hide the portrait from anyone's sight.

- **Section 1: Basil Confronts Dorian's Indifference** — Basil visits Dorian in distress over Sibyl Vane's tragic death, only to be shocked by Dorian's nonchalant attitude and his confession that he spent the previous evening at the opera.
- **Section 2: The Threat of Exhibition and Basil's Confession** — When Basil insists on exhibiting the portrait in Paris, a terrified Dorian refuses and demands to know why Basil previously vowed never to show it, prompting Basil to confess his intense artistic idolatry for him.
- **Section 3: Dorian Secures the Portrait in Secrecy** — Relieved that Basil's secret is merely an emotional confession rather than the discovery of the painting's curse, Dorian adamantly refuses to let him view or paint the portrait again before Basil bids him a regretful farewell.

## Chapter 10: Hiding the Portrait and the Poisonous Book

**Chapter summary:** Dorian Gray decides to conceal his cursed portrait permanently to hide the creeping evidence of his soul's corruption. He secures the key to his old childhood schoolroom at the top of the house from Mrs. Leaf and employs Mr. Hubbard, a frame-maker, and his assistant to move the canvas upstairs, covering it in a rich purple and gold Venetian embroidery. Afterward, Dorian receives a note and a newspaper from Lord Henry containing a red-marked paragraph reporting the inquest and verdict of death by misadventure regarding Sibyl Vane's suicide. Disturbed by the ugliness of reality, Dorian dismisses the tragedy and turns his attention to a mysterious, fascinating yellow book sent by Lord Henry—a French novel detailing the life of a Parisian youth consumed by artificial passions and sensory experiences. The book casts a powerful spell over Dorian, drawing him into a prolonged reverie. Absorbed in its pages, he loses track of time, arriving late to meet Lord Henry at his club, where he remarks that the book did not merely please him, but entirely fascinated him.

- **Section 1: Moving the Portrait to the Schoolroom** — Dorian decides to hide his portrait and obtains the key to the old schoolroom from Mrs. Leaf. He has Mr. Hubbard and his assistant carry the covered painting upstairs to the secure attic room, keeping the canvas hidden from their eyes.
- **Section 2: The Inquest and the Yellow Book** — Back in the library, Dorian reads a newspaper report about Sibyl Vane's inquest sent by Lord Henry and tries to push away the uncomfortable reminder of her death. He then becomes deeply absorbed in a strange, poisonous French novel sent by Lord Henry, reading it by the fading light until late into the evening.
- **Section 3: Late Arrival at the Club** — Dorian arrives late to his dinner meeting with Lord Henry at the club, explaining that he was entirely fascinated by the yellow book.

## Chapter 11: The Influence of the Poisonous Book

**Chapter summary:** Dorian Gray remains entirely captivated by the strange French novel sent by Lord Henry, finding in its pages a prefiguring type of his own life. While his physical beauty and youthful appearance remain miraculously unspotted, allowing him to easily charm society and set fashions, he secretly descends into degradation. He frequently visits his locked room to gloat over Basil Hallward's portrait, comparing his own fair face with the aging, evil canvas. Dorian embraces a new hedonism, exploring various intellectual and sensuous phases. He studies perfumes, barbaric music, rare jewels, embroideries, and ecclesiastical vestments as means of escaping his fears and seeking novel sensations. Despite dark rumors and whispered scandals regarding his mysterious absences and low associations in Whitechapel, society continues to welcome him due to his wealth, charm, and impeccable style. He often reflects on his lineage and identifies with historical figures of vice, feeling that history is the record of his own imagination.

- **Section 1: The Influence of the Book and Dorian's Secret Life** — Dorian remains enthralled by the French novel, which he views as the story of his own life, and while his outer beauty remains unblemished, he frequently visits his locked room to compare his youthful face with the aging portrait.
- **Section 2: Exploration of the New Hedonism and the Senses** — Dorian embraces a philosophy of new hedonism, turning his exquisite taste and curiosity toward the study of fashions, perfumes, barbaric musical instruments, rare jewels, historical embroideries, and ecclesiastical vestments.
- **Section 3: Scandals, Ancestry, and Historical Figures of Vice** — Despite growing social rumors and whispered scandals about his sordid associations, Dorian remains protected by his wealth and charm, viewing history and his own ancestors as a reflection of his complex, multi-faceted nature.

## Chapter 12: Basil Hallward Confronts Dorian Gray

**Chapter summary:** On the eve of his thirty-eighth birthday, Dorian Gray walks home through the fog and encounters Basil Hallward at the corner of Grosvenor Square and South Audley Street. Basil, who is on his way to catch a midnight train to Paris for a six-month stay, insists on visiting Dorian to speak with him seriously. Inside the library, Basil confronts Dorian about the horrific scandals and vile rumors circulating in London regarding his corrupting influence on young men like the Duke of Berwick, Lord Staveley, Adrian Singleton, and Lady Gwendolen, as well as his association with dens of vice. Dorian dismisses Basil's moralizing with contempt, arguing that society is hypocritical and full of slanderers. Distressed and bewildered, Basil begs Dorian to deny the charges and wonders if he even truly knows Dorian's soul. Provoked by Basil's statement that only God can see a man's soul, a defiant Dorian resolves to show Basil the secret, aged portrait he created, leading him upstairs to witness the true horror of his sin.

- **Section 1: Unexpected Meeting in the Fog** — Dorian walks home through a cold London fog on the eve of his birthday and unexpectedly encounters Basil Hallward, who is carrying a bag and heading to the midnight train for Paris. Basil insists on entering Dorian's house for a brief conversation before his departure.
- **Section 2: Basil Confronts Dorian with Scandals** — Basil seriously confronts Dorian about the dreadful rumors and ruined reputations of young men and women associated with him in London society. Dorian dismisses the accusations with contempt, blaming English hypocrisy and the chatter of the middle classes.
- **Section 3: The Decision to Show the Soul** — Driven to despair by Dorian's cold indifference, Basil asks if he truly knows Dorian and states that only God can see a man's soul. In a fit of pride and mockery, Dorian declares that Basil shall see his soul himself and invites him upstairs to look upon the hidden, sinful portrait.

## Chapter 13: Dorian Shows Basil the Portrait and Commits Murder

**Chapter summary:** Dorian Gray leads Basil Hallward up to the locked schoolroom and forces him to look at the cursed, aging portrait, which now bears a hideously debased and cruel expression. Horrified, Basil recognizes his own handiwork and realizes the truth of Dorian's corrupted soul. He urges Dorian to pray for forgiveness, but Dorian is overcome by a sudden, irrational hatred inspired by the grinning canvas. Seizing a knife, Dorian attacks Basil, stabbing him repeatedly until he is dead. Leaving the corpse seated at the table, Dorian methodically covers his tracks. He hides Basil's belongings in a secret press, establishes a false alibi by slipping out and returning later to query his sleepy valet about whether Basil has already left for Paris, and finally looks up the name of Alan Campbell in a directory, intending to enlist his help in disposing of the body.

- **Section 1: Basil Views the Horrific Portrait** — Dorian leads Basil up to the locked schoolroom and forces him to uncover the painting, revealing the hideous, aging face of his soul. Stunned and horrified, Basil recognizes his own work and begs Dorian to repent.
- **Section 2: The Murder of Basil Hallward** — Consumed by sudden hatred and a mad impulse, Dorian stabs Basil to death. He calmly leaves the corpse in the room, hides Basil's coat and bag, and prepares his alibi.
- **Section 3: Establishing an Alibi and Seeking Alan Campbell** — Dorian successfully tricks his valet Francis into believing Basil left for Paris at eleven. Left alone in the library, Dorian consults a directory to find Alan Campbell.

## Chapter 14: Blackmail and the Disposal of Basil's Body

**Chapter summary:** The morning after murdering Basil Hallward, Dorian Gray wakes up untroubled and tries to distract himself by reading Gautier's poetry, though he grows frantic waiting to hear from Alan Campbell. When Campbell arrives, he initially refuses to help Dorian, expressing absolute contempt and disgust. However, Dorian breaks Campbell's resistance by showing him a pre-written letter containing a dark secret, forcing Campbell to agree to chemically destroy Basil's body. Campbell orders his scientific apparatus from his laboratory and goes to the locked room to perform the gruesome task. Meanwhile, Dorian sends his servant Francis on an errand to Richmond to buy orchids. After spending hours on the horrifying work, a pale and composed Campbell finishes, leaves the house, and declares he never wants to see Dorian again. Dorian goes upstairs to find that the corpse has been completely eradicated, leaving only the lingering smell of nitric acid.

- **Section 1: Dorian's Morning and Anticipation** — Dorian wakes up peacefully the morning after the murder, distracts himself with Gautier's poems, and anxiously waits while sending a message to Alan Campbell.
- **Section 2: Confrontation and Blackmail** — Alan Campbell arrives and adamantly refuses to assist Dorian until Dorian forces his compliance by showing him a hidden letter containing a ruinous threat.
- **Section 3: Arranging the Laboratory Equipment** — Campbell has his chemical chest and instruments delivered to the house, and Dorian sends his servant Francis away on an errand to buy orchids.
- **Section 4: The Destruction of Basil's Remains** — Dorian covers the grotesque portrait and leaves Campbell alone in the schoolroom to destroy Basil's body, after which Campbell departs and leaves Dorian free.

## Chapter 15: Dorian Dines Out and Destroys Evidence

**Chapter summary:** Dorian Gray attends a dinner party at Lady Narborough’s house, maintaining a calm and graceful demeanor despite the inner turmoil of his recent crimes. During the meal, he sits near Lord Henry, who engages in witty banter about marriage and acquaintances. When Lord Henry casually questions Dorian about his late-night activities and the time he returned home, Dorian becomes visibly flustered and irritated. He cuts the evening short, abruptly excuses himself, and returns home. Once inside his locked library, Dorian builds a massive fire and spends three-quarters of an hour burning the coat and bag that belonged to the murdered Basil Hallward. Afterward, drawn by a compulsive craving, he unlocks a Florentine cabinet and inspects a Chinese lacquer box containing a waxy green paste. As midnight strikes, Dorian changes into common clothes, wraps a muffler around his throat, bribes a hansom cab driver with a sovereign, and drives rapidly away toward the river.

- **Section 1: Dinner at Lady Narborough's** — Dorian attends a small dinner party hosted by Lady Narborough, where he and Lord Henry converse with the guests and trade cynical remarks about marriage and society.
- **Section 2: Lord Henry's Inquiries and Dorian's Departure** — Over dinner and subsequent political talk, Lord Henry questions Dorian about his late return the previous night, making Dorian lose his nerve and decide to leave early.
- **Section 3: Burning Basil's Belongings and Leaving for the River** — Returning home, Dorian spends nearly an hour burning Basil Hallward's coat and bag in the library fire, inspects a Chinese box of green paste in his Florentine cabinet, and slips out into the night to take a cab toward the river.

## Chapter 16: Seeking Oblivion in London's Opium Dens

**Chapter summary:** Seeking to escape the agonizing memory of Basil Hallward's murder and find forgetfulness, Dorian Gray takes a hansom cab through the rainy London night to an infamous opium den near the docks. Inside, he encounters the ruined Adrian Singleton and reflects on the inescapable nature of his past mistakes. As he leaves and walks along the quay, Dorian is suddenly ambushed from behind by James Vane, who holds him at gunpoint and accuses him of driving Sibyl Vane to suicide eighteen years ago. Paralyzed with terror, Dorian saves his own life by tricking James into looking at his face under a gas-lamp; because Dorian's appearance remains that of a fresh youth, James believes he is mistaken and releases him in horror. Shortly after, a woman from the den approaches James and swears that Dorian is indeed the man called Prince Charming who ruined her life nearly two decades ago. When James realizes the truth and turns to pursue him, Dorian has already vanished into the night.

- **Section 1: A Journey to the Docks** — Dorian rides in a hansom cab through the rainy London night, reflecting on Lord Henry's philosophy of curing the soul through the senses as he heads toward an opium den to escape his guilt.
- **Section 2: Encountering Adrian Singleton** — Dorian enters the squalid opium den and encounters Adrian Singleton, reflecting on the destructive paths of his past before leaving amidst the insults of the patrons.
- **Section 3: Ambushed by James Vane** — While walking along the dark quay, Dorian is suddenly attacked and held at gunpoint by James Vane, who seeks revenge for Sibyl Vane's suicide eighteen years prior.
- **Section 4: Saved by Youthful Appearance** — Dorian tricks James Vane into looking at his youthful face under a gas-lamp, convincing James he has the wrong man; though a woman later reveals the truth, Dorian escapes.

## Chapter 17: Dorian Faints at the Conservatory After Seeing James Vane

**Chapter summary:** At his country estate Selby Royal, Dorian Gray entertains a house-party, including the pretty Duchess of Monmouth and Lord Henry Wotton. Lord Henry engages the duchess in witty banter about names, beauty, art, and the nature of love and pleasure, while Dorian flirts with her and confesses that he has always sought pleasure over happiness. Amidst the lively conversation and flirtation, Dorian goes to pick orchids for the duchess. Suddenly, a stifled groan and a heavy fall echo from the far end of the conservatory. Lord Henry and the guests rush over to find Dorian lying unconscious on the floor in a deathlike swoon. When Dorian regains consciousness, he trembles and asks if he is safe, tormented by the memory that caused his collapse. Although Lord Henry suggests he skip dinner, Dorian insists on attending because he fears being alone. At the dinner table, he puts on a wildly reckless display of gaiety, but is secretly gripped by terror, haunted by the realization that he saw the pale face of James Vane pressed against the conservatory window watching him.

- **Section 1: Conversation in the Conservatory** — Dorian and Lord Henry converse with the Duchess of Monmouth and other guests in the conservatory at Selby Royal about names, flowers, and the British character.
- **Section 2: Debating Art, Love, and Pleasure** — The duchess and Lord Henry debate art, society, and the pursuit of love, while Dorian admits to the duchess that he seeks pleasure rather than happiness.
- **Section 3: Flirting and Philosophical Banter** — Lord Henry and the duchess discuss flirtation and romanticism, and Dorian leaves to get orchids for her.
- **Section 4: Dorian's Collapse and Terror** — Dorian faints in the conservatory after seeing James Vane's face at the window, and later hides his terror behind reckless gaiety at dinner.

## Chapter 18: The Death of James Vane

**Chapter summary:** Dorian Gray spends days in his room at Selby Royal, paralyzed by the fear that James Vane is hunting him to avenge Sibyl. Convinced his terror is merely a morbid imagination, he ventures out to join a shooting party. During the hunt, Sir Geoffrey Clouston accidentally shoots and kills a beater hiding in the thicket. Deeply shaken, Dorian views the tragedy as a grim omen of his own doom. Later, the head-keeper, Thornton, informs Dorian that the dead man was an unidentified sailor with tattoos. ## Chapter 20: The Destruction of the Cursed Portrait

**Chapter summary:** Dorian Gray returns home on a warm night, reflecting on his recent attempt at a supposed reformation by sparing Hetty Merton. Troubled by his past, including Basil Hallward's murder and Alan Campbell's suicide, he laments the beauty and youth that ruined him and smashes the mirror Lord Henry once gave him. Hoping his good deed might have improved the portrait, Dorian goes upstairs to inspect it. Instead of finding redemption, he sees only increased cunning, hypocrisy, and spreading blood stains, realizing his renunciation stemmed merely from vanity and hypocrisy. Desperate to rid himself of the conscience-like evidence of his crimes, Dorian seizes the very knife he used to kill Basil Hallward and plunges it into the painting. Outside, passersby and a policeman hear a terrible cry from the house. Later, the servants force their way into the locked room and discover a magnificent portrait of Dorian in his youthful splendor, alongside a dead, withered, and wrinkled old man on the floor whom they recognize by his rings as Dorian Gray.

- **Section 1: Reflections on Youth and the Past** — Dorian walks home reflecting on Hetty Merton and his past corruption, eventually smashing a mirror in a fit of loathing for his beauty.
- **Section 2: Inspection of the Portrait** — Dorian reflects that he is safe from his past crimes, but when he inspects the portrait to see if his good deed has changed it, he finds it even more loathsome and marked with fresh blood.
- **Section 3: The Fatal Stab and Death** — Determined to destroy the portrait and the evidence of his past, Dorian stabs the painting with the knife that killed Basil, resulting in his own death and transformation.
